How I'd approach AI adoption at a large UK charity

Faizaan Saeed · built for a Change and Adoption Manager (AI) application

Switching AI tools on is the easy part. The real work is helping colleagues in very different jobs see what changes for them, get past what's stopping them, and still be using it well six months later. This page shows how I'd work that through, and you can try each step.

1 · Change impact and readiness assessment

Not every team needs the same thing at the same time. I'd score how much AI changes each group's day-to-day work (impact), then how ready they are against the five ADKAR elements: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement. They build in that order, so the place to start is the first one that's weak, not simply the lowest score. High impact plus low readiness is where support goes first. Move the sliders to try it.

4 · Measuring adoption and sustained behaviour change

Licences assigned is not adoption. I'd track leading indicators (early signs it's taking hold) alongside lagging ones (whether it stuck and made a difference), and use them to change what we're doing, not just to report.

Leading indicators

  • Active use: share of licensed people using it in a given week (from the tool's own usage reports), looked at by team and never used to rank individuals.
  • Depth of use: how many different tasks or apps people use it in, not just one-off chat.
  • Confidence: a two-question pulse ("I know when to use it", "I know what not to put in it").
  • Session and clinic attendance; questions raised with champions.

Lagging indicators

  • Sustained use at 90 days: still active three months after onboarding.
  • Value stories: specific, checked examples of time saved or quality improved.
  • Changed practice: teams that have rewritten a process around it.
  • Responsible use: data incidents, and whether people report them quickly.

5 · Responsible AI for a charity

Adoption and safety are one job, not two. If people aren't sure what's allowed, the careful ones stop using it and the careless ones carry on. These are the points I'd build into every intervention. The rules themselves belong to the data protection, information governance, safeguarding and fundraising compliance leads; my job would be making them clear and usable.

Supporter data

Supporters trust a charity with their details. Use only approved tools, use the minimum data a task needs, and never paste supporter records into personal or free AI accounts. Under UK GDPR, any new use of personal data has to be compatible with the purpose it was collected for.

Health data is special category

In a heart-health context it can appear anywhere: a supporter mentioning their own condition on a call, or a note on why they give. Under UK GDPR, health data is special category data: using it needs a lawful basis and a separate Article 9 condition, and a new use that is likely to be high risk needs a data protection impact assessment first. Rule of thumb for colleagues: if it reveals something about someone's health, check with the data protection team before it goes near an AI tool.

Safeguarding and judgement

AI can help draft and summarise, but decisions about people (a vulnerable supporter, a grant applicant, a volunteer) stay with people. A safeguarding concern always goes through the charity's normal safeguarding route, and is never left to an AI tool to triage or decide.

Check the output

AI can sound certain and be wrong, which matters most for health information shared publicly. "AI drafts, you decide": check facts against the source, and have clinical or medical content reviewed by the right experts before it goes out.

Fundraising copy is still fundraising

The Code of Fundraising Practice applies whoever, or whatever, wrote the first draft. An AI-drafted appeal still has to be accurate and not misleading, treat people in vulnerable circumstances fairly, and respect supporters' contact preferences. I'd have a named person sign off anything that goes out.

Copilot: tidy the house first

Microsoft 365 Copilot only surfaces organisational content that the person using it can already view. So it doesn't break permissions, but anything overshared on SharePoint or Teams becomes much easier to stumble on. Before scaling up, I'd ask Technology and information governance about a permissions and sensitivity-labelling check. It's an adoption risk as much as a technical one: one bad surprise can set trust back months.

About me, honestly

What I bring
